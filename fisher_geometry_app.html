From probability laws to geometry

A statistical model is a family of distributions. Its coordinates might be probabilities, means or standard deviations. The Fisher metric asks how much a small change in those coordinates changes the distribution in a way data can distinguish.

gᵢⱼ(θ) = Eθ[∂ᵢ log pθ(X) · ∂ⱼ log pθ(X)]
ds² = dθᵀ g(θ) dθ

One point, two views

The left chart uses familiar parameters. Its ellipses satisfy δθᵀgδθ = 0.2². Each ellipse shows tangent displacements of the same local Fisher length; it is a local approximation, not a finite confidence region. Near a boundary the ellipse may be clipped by the model domain.

The right view uses the same distributions in coordinates or an embedding where the geometry is easier to see. It shows a finite geodesic circle of radius 0.2 around the probe. The sphere is an isometric embedding; the coin angles are Euclidean coordinates; the normal disk is a conformal chart with a hyperbolic metric.

These are exact Levi–Civita curvatures, not estimates from a heatmap. In two dimensions R = 2K. Positive and negative curvature refer to intrinsic Riemannian geometry. Curvature alone does not measure the bumpiness of a loss function or establish whether an optimizer will struggle.

The square-root sphere

p ↦ (2√p₁, 2√p₂, 2√p₃)
d(A, B) = 2 arccos Σᵢ √(aᵢbᵢ)

Because Σpᵢ = 1, the image lies on a sphere of radius 2. Pulling back the Euclidean metric gives Σdpᵢ²/pᵢ. The shortest path is the great-circle arc in the positive octant. The plotted sphere is an orthographic projection: screen distances are not spherical distances. Drag it to rotate.

Two independent Bernoulli variables

θ₁ = 2 arcsin √p,   θ₂ = 2 arcsin √q
ds² = dθ₁² + dθ₂²

One observation contains both flips, with four joint probabilities (1−p)(1−q), (1−p)q, p(1−q), pq. Independence restricts the statistical model to two dimensions. The geodesic stays inside that independence model. A shorter great-circle path in the full four-category simplex could leave independence; it is a different optimization problem.

Normal distributions are hyperbolic

ds² = (dμ² + 2dσ²)/σ²
x = μ/√2,   y = σ
ds² = 2(dx² + dy²)/y²

The upper half-plane is therefore hyperbolic with K = −1/2. Its geodesics are vertical lines or semicircles orthogonal to y = 0 in (x, y). In (μ, σ), those semicircles are stretched horizontally. The disk shows the same metric in conformal coordinates, where ds² = 8|dw|²/(1−|w|²)². Points near the disk rim are far apart in the hyperbolic metric.

Three routes need not agree

The Fisher route minimizes Riemannian length and is parameterized at constant Fisher speed. The parameter line interpolates the coordinates shown on the left. Exponential interpolation is straight in natural parameters: log-odds for the coin model and (μ/σ², −1/2σ²) for normals; categorical probabilities follow normalized geometric interpolation.

Exponential interpolation is an e-geodesic, which refers to the exponential connection. The curvature reported here is for the Levi–Civita connection. An exponential family can be dually flat while its Levi–Civita geometry has nonzero curvature.

KL, local distance and sample size

KL(Pθ ∥ Pθ₊δ) = ½ δᵀg(θ)δ + O(‖δ‖³)

KL is directional and is not a metric. Half the squared Fisher distance agrees with it to second order near the starting point, but not in general over a finite separation. The graph follows the selected route; its gold curve uses the shortest distance from A to each probe distribution, rather than the length already traveled along a longer route.

For n independent repetitions, gₙ = ng, KLₙ = n KL, dₙ = √n d and Kₙ = K/n. In the coin model, a repetition is a complete pair of flips. The right view auto-fits its coordinate scale; the distributions themselves do not change when n changes.

Three-dimensional models

The volume views keep all three parameters visible. Drag to rotate, use the rotation slider, or focus the volume and use the arrow keys. The gold glyph is a tangent ellipsoid of Fisher length 0.2, rather than a finite confidence region. Its axes can extend beyond a probability boundary; very long axes are truncated and explicitly labeled. Dashed gold axes mark numerical null directions where a bounded equal-length ellipsoid no longer exists.

Three independent coins have eight joint outcomes and a flat Fisher-angle cube. Four outcomes have three degrees of freedom: their probability chart is a tetrahedron, while their square-root image lies in R⁴ on S³ of radius 2√n. A tetrahedron in this lab is a coordinate chart, not a Euclidean isometric realization of that curved manifold.

Products and sectional curvature

gP×Q = gP ⊕ gQ
d²P×Q = d²P + d²Q

For the coin–normal product, one observation includes both independent variables. The Fisher metric is n[dθ² + (dμ² + 2dσ²)/σ²]. The normal tangent plane has K = −1/(2n); mixed planes spanned by a coin direction and a normal direction have K = 0. The tilt control uses the plane spanned by eμ and cos(α)eσ + sin(α)eθ, where the e vectors are Fisher-orthonormal. It gives K = −cos²(α)/(2n).

In dimension three, scalar curvature is twice the sum of sectional curvatures over the three coordinate planes of an orthonormal frame. It is 0 for three independent coins, 3/(2n) for four outcomes, and −1/n for the coin–normal product. The two-dimensional relation R = 2K does not apply to a general three-dimensional model.

Mixtures and invisible directions

f(x) = w φ(x − μ₁) + (1 − w) φ(x − μ₂)
gᵢⱼ = n ∫ (∂ᵢf ∂ⱼf)/f dx

Both component variances are fixed at one. The component label is unobserved, so this is a mixture rather than a product. Exchanging component labels preserves the observed density. At μ₁ = μ₂ the Fisher matrix has rank one: its only first-order identifiable direction is w dμ₁ + (1 − w)dμ₂. Its two null directions do not imply that the entire nearby model has only one dimension; higher-order changes can distinguish separated components.

The mixture display follows a straight line in (w, μ₁, μ₂). It does not claim that this is a Fisher geodesic. The length of a parameter path may be positive even when its endpoints represent the same density. No mixture distance or curvature estimate is displayed. Where the matrix loses rank, it does not define a Riemannian metric on this three-parameter chart.

Numerical details and saving

In the regular surface and volume models, distances, geodesics, metrics and curvatures use closed forms. Comparison lengths use 600-segment midpoint integration, so small discrepancies can occur near coincident paths. Normal density curves are displayed over a finite x window. Endpoints stay strictly inside the regular model domain. The mixture Fisher matrix and KL use 640-panel Simpson quadrature over the union of component means expanded by nine standard deviations. Volume comparison-path lengths use 500-segment midpoint integration. Numerical rank uses max(10⁻¹⁰, 10⁻⁸λmax) as the eigenvalue tolerance. Save records all seven model setups and the current probe; Open restores the experiment paused. The offline HTML includes the same setup and has no external dependencies.
