Begin with zero depth

Open Fusion target. The two camera views initially match, except that one eye receives a horizontal stroke and the other a vertical stroke. Bring the two inner circles together into a middle circle. A complete + in the middle shows that the two eye views are being combined. The two outer copies can remain visible.

For parallel viewing, keep the pair small and let your gaze relax past the screen. For cross-eye viewing, a fingertip held between you and the screen can help you bring the inner targets together; then try focusing on the middle target. These methods do not work equally well for everyone.

Once you have a steady middle image, choose Gentle depth. The amber dot is nearer and the teal dot farther than the reference dot. Then continue with two linked fibers. Keep the scene still while finding the image; moving beads and rotation can wait.

Why image spacing matters

For parallel viewing, corresponding features separated by more than your eye spacing require the eyes to diverge. The viewer flags that geometry using your chosen eye separation. This is a geometric check, not a guarantee of comfortable fusion at smaller separations. Use Smaller pair to reduce the width and gap together.

At zero local camera disparity, a free-viewed pair can still appear behind the screen in parallel mode or in front of it in cross-eye mode. In anaglyph mode the zero-disparity reference is on the screen. The reference-distance readout uses the simple ray geometry Z = ID/(I − S), where I is eye spacing, D is viewing distance, and S is signed image-center separation.

The camera construction

The cameras stay parallel and use shifted frusta. For a point (x,y,z), eye position e, reference distance D and focal scale f, the local projected coordinates are:

u = f[(x − e)/(D − z) + e/D]
v = fy/(D − z)

The vertical coordinate is identical for both eyes. At z = 0 the horizontal coordinates match too. Depth controls cap the horizontal camera-view shift using the scene bound. Cross-eye mode swaps the camera views; it does not mirror the scene. Anaglyph uses luminance in the red and cyan channels.

Alternatives and saving

Wiggle moves a single viewpoint between the two eye positions, with a threefold motion gain. It provides motion parallax, rather than simultaneous binocular stereo. Orbit lets you drag and inspect the geometry directly. Neither requires you to fuse two images.

Save records the scene, view, animation phase and calibration. Open restores the setup paused. Offline HTML contains the same setup and has no external dependencies. An exported image should be displayed at the intended physical scale if you want to preserve its free-viewing arrangement. Fusion is something you assess with your eyes; the viewer cannot detect it.

Circles above points on a sphere

Write a point of S³ as (z₁,z₂) in C², with |z₁|² + |z₂|² = 1. This viewer uses the Hopf map

H(z₁,z₂) = (2 Re(z̄₁z₂), 2 Im(z̄₁z₂), |z₁|² − |z₂|²).

Multiplying both complex coordinates by the same phase eⁱᵗ leaves H unchanged. The points (eⁱᵗz₁,eⁱᵗz₂) trace one circular fiber in S³. Distinct fibers are linked once. The dots in the longitude/latitude chart are base points on S²; highlighting a dot selects its fiber.

Bringing S³ into view

With (x₀,x₁,x₂,x₃) = (Re z₁, Im z₁, Re z₂, Im z₂), stereographic projection from the pole (+1,0,0,0) is

(x₀,x₁,x₂,x₃) ↦ (x₁,x₂,x₃)/(1 − x₀).

A fiber avoiding the projection pole becomes a circle in R³. The one fiber through the pole becomes a straight line. Choose Circle & exceptional line to see that case. A line is the image of the fiber minus the removed projection point; it is not a broken Hopf circle.

The viewer clips the unbounded R³ picture to a sphere of radius 3.8 before uniformly fitting the chosen geometry into the view. Large circles can therefore have clipped ends. Changing presets refits the visible geometry. Tube thickness is a drawing aid, not part of the Hopf map.

What the presets sample

One torus uses base points around the equator. Three nested tori use three latitudes. The belt samples latitudes with −0.8 < n_z < 0.8, avoiding the projection singularity. The first scene contains only two fibers, so the linking is easy to follow.

Beads follow the common S³ phase and are transformed with the same 3D rotation as the fibers. They can move nonuniformly in the stereographic picture and disappear outside the clipping window. The fiber itself stays fixed under a common phase change.
